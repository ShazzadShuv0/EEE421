Slides 3–5 Introduction and classification

What a rectifier does

The grid delivers AC because it travels well; most equipment needs DC. A rectifier makes current flow one way, a filter smooths the pulses, and a regulator holds the final level.

The switch decides what kind of converter you get. Diodes conduct whenever they are forward-biased, so their output is fixed. Thyristors (SCRs) are forward-biased and still wait: they turn on only when a gate pulse arrives. Delaying that pulse by the firing angle α is how every circuit on this page controls its DC output. PWM rectifiers with MOSFETs or IGBTs switch thousands of times per cycle for very clean DC, and appear at the end of the lecture.

Slides 6–7 Principle of phase control

Single-phase half wave, R load

One thyristor in series with a resistor. It blocks until the gate fires at α, conducts until the supply reaches zero at π, then blocks the whole negative half.

  1. 0 → αForward blocking. The anode is positive but there is no gate pulse, so io = 0 and the full supply appears across the SCR: vT = vs.
  2. α → πConducting. vo = vs, vT ≈ 0, and io = vs/R has the same shape as vo.
  3. At πThe current reaches zero together with the supply, so the SCR turns off by itself (natural, or line, commutation).
  4. π → 2πReverse blocking: vT = vs < 0. The next pulse comes at 2π + α.
Vdc = (Vm / 2π)(1 + cos α)
Vrms = (Vm / 2) √[(1/π)(π − α + ½ sin 2α)]
Peak reverse voltage Vm; peak forward blocking Vm sin α for α ≤ 90°, Vm beyond.
  • Sweep α and watch the amber forward-blocking area in vT grow: that is the voltage the SCR must hold off before firing.
  • Set α = 0: the circuit behaves exactly like a diode rectifier.
Slide 8 Half wave converter with inductive load

Single-phase half wave, R-L load

The inductor resists change in current, so current builds slowly after α and keeps flowing past π. The SCR stays on into the negative half-cycle until the current dies at the extinction angle β.

  1. α → peakCurrent rises; vL = vs − Ri > 0 and the inductor stores energy.
  2. peak → πvs has fallen below Ri, so vL < 0: the inductor starts giving energy back while the supply is still positive.
  3. π → βArea B. vs is negative, yet the inductor holds the SCR on, so vo follows vs below zero. Part of the stored energy heats R, part returns to the supply.
  4. β → 2π + αSCR off; vT steps straight to the negative supply voltage.
io = (Vm/Z)[sin(ωt − φ) − sin(α − φ) e−(ωt − α)/tan φ], Z = √(R² + ω²L²), φ = tan−1(ωL/R)
Vdc = (Vm / 2π)(cos α − cos β)
β has no closed form; the simulator solves io(β) = 0 numerically.

Why does the inductor stop charging before π? It charges while vs > Ri, not while vs > 0. At π the supply is zero but Ri is still positive, so the current peak always comes earlier. Only a pure inductor (R = 0) peaks exactly at π; drag φ toward 89° to see the peak slide right.

  • In the vL panel the yellow and red areas are always equal: an inductor's average voltage over a cycle is zero.
  • Raise φ and watch area B grow and Vdc fall.
Slide 9 Half wave converter with R-L-E load

Adding a back-EMF: battery or DC motor

With a counter-EMF E in the load, the SCR is forward-biased only while vs > E. That sets a minimum useful firing angle.

θ1 = sin−1(E / Vm)
Fire at α < θ1 and the SCR is still reverse-biased (E > vs), so it will not turn on. Conduction can last at most until π − θ1 plus whatever extra the inductance provides.

No simulator for this one: the R-L sheet above shows the same mechanism with E = 0.

Slide 10 Inductive load with freewheeling diode

Half wave, R-L load with freewheeling diode

A diode across the load gives the inductor's current somewhere to go when the supply reverses. The output is clamped at zero instead of going negative, so area B disappears.

  1. α → πMode 1: SCR conducts, vo = vs, the freewheeling diode FD is reverse-biased.
  2. At πvo tries to go negative, FD turns on and takes the current; the SCR is reverse-biased and turns off at once.
  3. π → 2π + αMode 2: current circulates through FD, R and L and decays exponentially. All the stored energy is burned in R; none goes back to the supply.
Vdc = (Vm / 2π)(1 + cos α), the same as an R load and independent of L
With large L the current has not decayed when the next pulse arrives: conduction becomes continuous.
  • Push φ above about 75° and the mode card switches to continuous.
  • Move φ while watching Vdc: it does not change.

Single-phase half-wave circuits give one pulse of load current per cycle: one-pulse converters. Practice: Bimbhra, Exercises 6.1 to 6.4, p. 241.

Slide 14 Single-phase full-wave mid-point converter

Mid-point converter, R load

A centre-tapped transformer gives two equal, opposite half-windings. T1 serves the positive half-cycle, T2 the negative one, so the load gets two pulses per cycle.

  1. α → πT1 on, vo = van. The common cathode sits at van, so T2 sees vbn − van = −2van.
  2. π → π + αNothing conducts; each SCR sees only its own half-winding.
  3. π + α → 2πT2 on, vo = vbn. Now T1 sees 2van, dipping to −2Vm.
Vdc = (Vm / π)(1 + cos α)
Vrms = (Vm / √2) √[(1/π)(π − α + ½ sin 2α)]
Peak reverse voltage per SCR: 2Vm, the main cost of using only two thyristors.
  • Watch vT1 step down to twice the depth the moment T2 fires; the dotted line marks −2Vm.
Slide 14 Single-phase two-pulse converter, inductive load

Mid-point converter, R-L load

The inductor keeps each SCR conducting past π. If the next SCR fires before the current dies, the hand-over is direct and the load current never stops.

  1. α → πT1 on, the supply drives the load.
  2. π → π + αvan is negative, but the inductor holds T1 on: vo dips below zero.
  3. At π + αT2 fires, connects the cathode to the positive vbn and reverse-biases T1: line commutation.
Continuous when α < φ: Vdc = (2Vm / π) cos α
Discontinuous when α ≥ φ: Vdc = (Vm / π)(cos α − cos β)
In continuous mode the off SCR always sees 2vs, including forward blocking up to 2Vm sin α.
  • Hold φ = 70° and sweep α: the mode flips to discontinuous exactly at α = 70°.
  • With a passive R-L load, α > 90° is always discontinuous, so Vdc cannot stay negative.
Slide 15 Single-phase bridge full converter

Bridge full converter, four SCRs

T1 + T2 fire together at α, T3 + T4 at π + α. Output voltage and load current match the mid-point converter, but each SCR only sees Vm and no centre tap is needed.

  1. T1 + T2 onvo = +vs, is = +io.
  2. T3 + T4 onvo = −vs, is = −io. Load current keeps its direction; supply current flips.
  3. At π + αThe incoming pair reverse-biases the outgoing pair by vs.
Vdc = (2Vm / π) cos α (continuous)
Two-quadrant: rectifier for α < 90°, inverter for α > 90° when the load contains a source such as a motor's back-EMF. is lags vs by α, so the displacement factor is cos α.
  • Compare vT1 here with the mid-point sheet: the peak is Vm, not 2Vm.
  • Watch the positive part of vT3 just before π + α: forward blocking, waiting for the gate.
Slide 16 Single-phase bridge semiconverter

Bridge semiconverter, two SCRs and two diodes

Half the bridge is diodes, which give the inductor a freewheeling path. The output can never go negative, so this is a one-quadrant converter.

  1. α → πT1 + D1 conduct: vo = vs, is = +io.
  2. π → π + αFreewheeling: through Dm if fitted, otherwise through T1 + D2 in one leg. vo = 0, is = 0.
  3. π + α → 2πT2 + D2 conduct: vo = −vs > 0, is = −io.
Vdc = (Vm / π)(1 + cos α)
The supply current has zero gaps, so its fundamental lags by about α/2 instead of α: a better input power factor than the full converter.
  • Toggle Dm off: vo, io and is stay the same, but each SCR and diode now conducts a full 180°.

Practice: Bimbhra, Example 6.26 and Exercise 6.8, p. 242.

Slides 19–21 Three-phase rectifiers using diodes

Three-phase diode rectifiers

With three phases, the output is simply the highest available voltage at each instant. No gate is needed: the diode on the most positive phase always wins.

In the common-cathode half-wave circuit the output follows the top of the three phase voltages: three pulses per cycle. The common-anode version follows the bottom. Put both together and you get the six-pulse bridge, whose output is the largest line-to-line voltage at every instant. Each diode switches at the natural commutation points where two phase curves cross, 30° after each zero crossing. Those crossings become the zero reference for α in the controlled versions below.

Slide 22 Three-phase half-wave controlled rectifier, three-pulse converter

Three-phase half-wave controlled, R load

Three SCRs with a common cathode. α is measured from the 30° crossover, not from the phase zero crossing, because that is the earliest moment an SCR is forward-biased against the one already conducting.

  1. α = 0T1 fires at 30°, T2 at 150°, T3 at 270°; each conducts 120° and the output tracks the highest phase.
  2. 0 < α ≤ 30°Still continuous. Being forward-biased does not turn T2 on; it waits for its pulse at 150° + α.
  3. α > 30°The conducting phase crosses zero before the next pulse, so vo drops to zero in gaps.
α ≤ 30°: Vdc = (3√3 Vm / 2π) cos α = 0.827 Vm cos α
α > 30°: Vdc = (3Vm / 2π)[1 + cos(α + 30°)]
  • Set α = 30° and look at 180°: the output just touches zero. That is the boundary between continuous and discontinuous.
Primer for slides 23–30 Where line voltages come from

How van − vcn makes a sine wave

Bridge outputs are line voltages, the vertical gap between two phase curves. Two curves can both be falling while the gap between them grows.

The slope of vac is the slope of van minus the slope of vcn. Between 90° and 120°, van is flat near its peak while vcn falls fast, so the gap widens. At 120° the slopes match and vac peaks at √3 Vm. After that van falls faster and the gap closes.

vac = van − vcn = √3 Vm sin(ωt − 30°)
Slides 23–27 Three-phase full-wave semiconverter

Three-phase semiconverter, highly inductive load

The top row is controlled thyristors, the bottom row plain diodes. The output is (thyristor's phase) − (most negative phase), so only half of each line-voltage pair moves with α.

  1. α ≤ 60°Six-pulse operation: vo never reaches zero and Dm stays off. Each SCR conducts 120°.
  2. α > 60°Three-pulse operation: vo would go negative, so Dm freewheels the load current and vo = 0. Each SCR conducts 180° − α.
Vdc = (3√3 Vm / 2π)(1 + cos α)
Load current assumed constant (Io). Output never reverses, so no inverter mode.
  • Set α = 90° to match the slide's example and watch Dm carry the current in the gaps.
  • Watch ia: the positive block shifts with α, the negative block from D2 does not.
Slides 28–30 Three-phase full-wave full converter

Three-phase full converter, six SCRs

Both rows are controlled. Thyristors are numbered in firing order, 60° apart, so the output stays six-pulse at every α and can go negative.

  1. Pairs5-6, 6-1, 1-2, 2-3, 3-4, 4-5: one top and one bottom SCR always conduct; every 60° one hands over.
  2. Gate pulses120° wide (or double pulses 60° apart) so both SCRs of a pair have a gate signal at start-up.
  3. α > 90°Inverter mode: average voltage reverses while current keeps its direction, if the DC side has a source.
Vdc = (3√3 Vm / π) cos α = 1.654 Vm cos α
ia is a quasi-square wave, +Io for 120°, zero for 60°, −Io for 120°, shifted by α.
  • Set α = 60° to match the slide, then push past 90° and watch Vdc change sign.
Slides 31–35 DC-side harmonics filtering

Smoothing what comes out

The output is a DC average with a ripple on top. Its lowest ripple frequency is the pulse number times the mains frequency, which is why more pulses mean easier filtering.

fr = p · fmains (50 Hz mains: 50, 100, 150 and 300 Hz for p = 1, 2, 3, 6)
LC resonance f0 = 1 / (2π√LC), chosen so f0 ≪ fr
FilterArrangementBest for
InductorL in series; blocks AC ripple, passes DCHigh load current
CapacitorC across the load; bypasses rippleLow load current
L-sectionSeries L then shunt COutput nearly independent of load
π-sectionC, series L, COutput near peak voltage, excellent ripple rejection
ActiveSenses ripple and injects the oppositeCompact, lower-power systems
Slides 36–39 Effect of the rectifier on the AC mains

What the rectifier does to the supply

Switching draws a non-sinusoidal current: a fundamental with harmonics on top. You have already seen it as is in the full-converter simulators.

Higher pulse numbers cut distortion: the lecture quotes a line-current THD of 28.45% for a 6-pulse rectifier and 9.14% for 12-pulse. Preventive fixes are multi-pulse converters with suitable transformer connections, active front ends that use PWM to draw near-sinusoidal current in phase with the voltage, and balancing single-phase loads across phases. Remedial fixes are tuned passive filters and active filters that inject an equal and opposite harmonic current.
